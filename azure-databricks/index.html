    One kitchen for all your data

    What Databricks is, why it exists, and where it fits in Azure.

    Photo: Florian Hirzinger - www.fh-ap.com · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    Your company keeps sales in one database, website clicks in log files and customer chats in another app. Then the boss asks: "Which customers are about to leave us?" Where do you even start?

    That question needs all three kinds of data, joined together, cleaned, and maybe fed to a machine-learning model. The data is too big for one laptop, and four different teams need to work on it.

    exists to solve exactly this: one place where engineers, analysts and data scientists work on the same data, with as many computers as the job needs.

    The big idea

    A rented, fully equipped kitchen

    Think of Databricks as a professional kitchen you rent by the hour. The stoves are computers. The pantry is cloud storage. The head chef and line cooks are , software that splits big jobs across many machines.

    You bring the recipe (your code or SQL). Databricks brings the kitchen, switches it on when you need it and off when you don't.

    Where it came from

    Built by the people who built Spark

    Apache Spark began as a research project at UC Berkeley. Its creators later started the company Databricks. Microsoft's documentation notes that several key open-source projects were originally created by Databricks employees: Apache Spark and Structured Streaming, Delta Lake, MLflow and OpenSharing.

    On Azure, Databricks is a native Azure service. You create it in the Azure portal, pay through your Azure subscription, sign in with and connect it to your Azure storage and networks.

    Bring the recipe. Databricks brings the kitchen, the cooks and the pantry.

    Chapter 2: Lake, warehouse, lakehouse →

    Beginner02

    A house built on the lake

    Data lake vs data warehouse vs lakehouse, and why the industry merged them.

    Photo: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

    For twenty years, companies kept two copies of their data: one tidy and expensive, one messy and cheap. Then someone asked: why not build one good house right on the lake?

    🏬

    A shop with neat, labelled shelves. Data is cleaned and shaped before it goes in, so reports are fast and trusted.

    • ✅ Fast, reliable SQL
    • ❌ Expensive storage
    • ❌ Struggles with images, logs, free text
    🏞️

    A huge lake where any file can flow in: CSV, JSON, images, logs. Cheap and flexible, but easy to turn into a swamp.

    • ✅ Cheap, any kind of data
    • ✅ Great for AI
    • ❌ No safe updates, hard to trust
    🏡

    Keep everything in cheap, open files on the lake, then add a "house": transactions, tables, permissions and speed.

    • ✅ One copy for BI and AI
    • ✅ Open file formats
    • ✅ Reliable updates and governance
    How the "house" works

    Open files plus a smart layer on top

    In a lakehouse, the data itself sits in cloud storage as files, an open format that stores data column by column. Anyone's tools can read it.

    On top sits a table format, , which keeps a log of every change. That log is what makes the files behave like a proper database table, with safe updates and history. Databricks also supports Apache Iceberg tables. Then adds names, permissions and lineage.

    Inside the data centerSTORAGE HALL · ROW S

    Your lakehouse files live on storage servers: machines packed with dozens of drives, in their own rows of the data center. Azure keeps several copies of every file, so one broken disk never loses your data.

    🏢
    Where it lives
    In the storage halls of your Azure region. is built on Azure Blob Storage, so your Parquet and Delta files sit in the same storage system as millions of other customers' files.
    🖥️
    The machines
    Storage servers, typically packed with many large hard disks for capacity and some SSDs for speed, plus fast network cards. Microsoft doesn't publish exact models.
    🔀
    The physical journey
    A query reads files from storage servers, across the data center network, into the memory of your cluster's VMs. Columnar Parquet means only the needed columns travel.
    ⚡
    Power & heat
    Spinning disks draw only a few watts each, but a hall with tens of thousands of them adds up, and they need steady, cool air.
    📏
    Scale
    With , Microsoft copies your data synchronously across three or more availability zones. Locally redundant storage (LRS) keeps the copies inside one data center.
    🛠️
    When something breaks
    Drives fail every day in a big hall. LRS protects against drive, server and rack failures: Azure reads another copy and quietly rebuilds the lost one.
    👂
    Feel it
    The storage rows hum lower than the compute rows: thousands of drives spinning, little activity lights flickering like a starfield.

    • Warehouses are tidy and fast but costly; lakes are cheap and flexible but messy.

    Chapter 3: The architecture →

    Beginner03

    Two planes, one platform

    Control plane, compute plane, workspaces, and what lands in your Azure subscription.

    Photo: Xpda · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

    When you click "Run" in a notebook, two very different sets of machines get involved: some run by Databricks, some running in your own Azure subscription. Knowing which is which is the first thing an architect learns.

    Azure Databricks is split into a and a . Think of a restaurant: the control plane is the front office that takes bookings, keeps the menus and tells the kitchen what to cook. The compute plane is the kitchen where the cooking, your data processing, actually happens.

    The pieces

    Account, workspaces, metastore

    At the top sits your Databricks account: users, billing, policies. Under it you create , each in one Azure region, where teams keep notebooks, jobs and dashboards.

    Next to them sits a , the shared catalog of tables and models. You can attach one metastore to many workspaces in the same region, so everyone sees the same data, with the same permissions.

    Two kinds of compute

    Classic vs serverless

    : the VMs are created inside your Azure subscription and virtual network. You choose sizes, you see them, and you pay Azure for them.

    : the machines run in a serverless compute plane inside the Databricks account, in the same Azure region as your workspace. They start in seconds and you never manage them.

    What lands in your subscription

    The managed resource group

    When you create a classic workspace, Azure also creates a managed resource group in your subscription. It holds the workspace storage account and, unless you bring your own network, a virtual network for the cluster VMs.

    Microsoft's docs are blunt about it: don't delete or modify the workspace storage. A workspace depends on it, and if it is deleted, the workspace cannot be recovered.

    Two workspace types

    Classic and serverless workspaces

    Classic workspaces (shown as "Hybrid" in the Azure portal) have a workspace storage account in your subscription and can run both classic and serverless compute.

    Serverless workspaces use fully managed default storage and serverless compute only, and can still connect to your own storage accounts for your data.

    Inside the data centerTWO WORLDS · ONE REGION

    Behind the two "planes" are two groups of real machines in the same Azure region. Databricks' control-plane services and serverless pools run in Databricks' own Azure account; your classic cluster VMs run on servers billed to your subscription.

    🏢
    Where it lives
    Both live in the region you chose for the workspace. Serverless compute is created in the same region as your workspace's classic compute plane.
    🖥️
    The machines
    Every cluster node is a : a slice of a physical rack server, carved out by a hypervisor. Your VMs and other customers' VMs may share the same building, but are isolated from each other.
    🔀
    The physical journey
    With , your cluster VMs have no public IPs and open no inbound ports. Each cluster dials out to a relay in the control plane over HTTPS (port 443) and gets its work through that connection.
    ⚡
    Power & heat
    Control-plane services are small and steady. The big power draw is the compute: every running worker VM is a share of a server burning watts until you stop it.
    📏
    Scale
    One region typically hosts many thousands of servers. Your 10-node cluster is a tiny tenant among them.
    🛠️
    When something breaks
    If a VM's host server dies, Azure moves or recreates VMs on another host, and Databricks replaces lost worker nodes. Your notebooks and jobs live in the control plane, so they survive.
    👂
    Feel it
    Picture two neighbouring halls of identical racks: one full of Databricks' shared machines, one hosting your cluster. Same hum, same cold air, different owners.

    Secure cluster connectivity ("No Public IP") is the default: in the Azure portal it's set to Yes when you create a workspace, and newer ARM template versions treat it as on unless you turn it off.

    Chapter 3 recap

    • The control plane (web app, scheduler, compute manager) runs in the Databricks account.
    • Classic compute runs VMs in your subscription and VNet; serverless runs in Databricks' account, same region.
    • Unity Catalog metastores sit at account level and can be shared by workspaces in one region.
    • Classic workspaces add a managed resource group with workspace storage: never delete it.

    Pick the right engine

    All-purpose, jobs, serverless, SQL warehouses, Photon and autoscaling, and how to choose.

    Photo: Victor Grigas · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    You wouldn't hire a bus to deliver one pizza, or a scooter to move house. Databricks gives you several kinds of compute for exactly the same reason.

    "Compute" just means the machines that run your work. The right choice depends on three things: what kind of work it is, how much control you need, and whether you care more about fast starts or the lowest price.

    ⚡

    For notebooks, jobs and pipelines. Starts in seconds, scales itself, and Databricks manages the machines. You pay DBUs only.

    🧑‍🍳

    All-purpose (classic)

    A you create for interactive work. Shared by a team, or dedicated to one person. You pick VM sizes, libraries and settings.

    🗓️

    Jobs compute (classic)

    Created just for one job run and shut down when it ends. Cheaper DBU rate than all-purpose; ideal for scheduled pipelines.

    📊

    Built for SQL and dashboards. Serverless (starts in seconds) or classic. is always on.

    🏊

    Instance pools

    A few idle VMs kept warm, so classic clusters start faster.

    Two access modes

    Standard or Dedicated?

    Classic compute comes in two access modes. Standard (formerly "shared") lets many users share one cluster safely. Databricks isolates each user's code with a technology called Lakeguard.

    Dedicated (formerly "single user") belongs to one user or group. Use it for machine-learning runtimes, GPUs, or workloads that need low-level access.

    Two speed-ups

    Photon and autoscaling

    is Databricks' native engine written in C++. It processes data in big column batches instead of row by row. It is on for serverless and SQL warehouses, and enabled by default for classic compute too.

    adds workers when the job is busy and removes them when it is quiet, between a minimum and maximum you set. Add auto-termination so idle clusters switch themselves off.

    Inside the data centerCOMPUTE ROW · E-SERIES

    Every cluster node you choose is a real Azure virtual machine, a slice of a rack server. The VM name tells you what kind of server slice you get: balanced, memory-heavy, disk-heavy, or packed with GPUs.

    🏢
    Where it lives
    In the compute rows of your Azure region's data halls, in racks of identical servers. For classic compute they are billed to your subscription; serverless uses Databricks' own pool.
    🖥️
    The machines
    D-series (general purpose; Ddsv5: 2–96 vCPUs, 8–384 GiB RAM, local SSD). E-series (memory-optimised; Edsv5: 2–104 vCPUs, 16–672 GiB) for joins and big aggregations. F-series (compute-optimised; Fsv2: 2–72 vCPUs, high clock speeds). L-series (storage-optimised; Lsv3: up to 10 local NVMe disks of 1.92 TiB) for heavy caching. N-series (GPU; ND H100 v5: 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs per VM) for deep learning.
    🔀
    The physical journey
    Your code is sent from the control plane to the driver VM, which hands tasks to worker VMs. Each worker pulls data from storage across the network into its RAM and CPU caches.
    ⚡
    Power & heat
    A busy general-purpose server typically draws several hundred watts; an 8-GPU server can draw over 10 kW. Pick bigger machines only when the work needs them.
    📏
    Scale
    The "d" in Ddsv5 and Edsv5 means a local temporary SSD, which Databricks uses for its disk cache (Chapter 15). Recent sizes run on Intel Xeon Platinum processors such as Ice Lake and Emerald Rapids.
    🛠️
    When something breaks
    If a worker VM's host fails, Databricks replaces the node and Spark reruns the lost tasks elsewhere. If the driver fails, the cluster restarts; your job's retry policy takes over.
    👂
    Feel it
    Walk down a compute row and the air coming out of the back of the racks is noticeably warm, while the cold aisle in front feels like an open fridge.

    • Serverless: fastest start, no machines to manage. Classic: full control, VMs in your subscription.

    Beginner06

    A kitchen full of cooks

    Driver and executors, partitions, transformations vs actions, lazy evaluation and the dreaded shuffle.

    Photo: Victor Grigas · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    A restaurant gets an order for 10,000 sandwiches by noon. One cook would need a week. Twenty cooks, each with a stack of bread, finish before lunch. That is the whole idea behind Spark.

    A Spark cluster is like a team of cooks splitting one big order so it's ready faster. The is the head chef: it reads your code and makes the plan. The are the line cooks: they do the actual work, each on its own slice of the data.

    🍕

    Your data is cut into slices, like a pizza. Each slice can be worked on by a different core at the same time.

    🧾

    One task = one slice processed by one executor core. A 2-core executor runs 2 tasks at once.

    📝

    filter, select, join, groupBy: recipe steps that describe what to do, but do nothing yet.

    🔔

    count, show, write: "Service!" The moment Spark actually starts cooking.

    Lazy on purpose

    Spark waits, then plans

    Spark uses . When you write a transformation, it just notes it down. Only when you call an action does it look at the whole recipe and find the fastest route, like a delivery driver who waits for all the orders before planning the trip.

    That's why .filter() before a join can be pushed down into the file read, so less data ever leaves storage.

    PySpark
    orders = spark.read.table("main.sales.orders")   # nothing runs
big = orders.filter("amount > 1000")            # still nothing
by_city = big.groupBy("city").count()          # still nothing

by_city.explain()   # show the plan Spark made
by_city.show()      # ACTION: now it runs
    The expensive part

    The shuffle

    Some steps need data from many slices at once. To count orders per city, every row for "Mumbai" must end up on the same executor. Spark moves rows across the network so they meet. That move is a .

    Shuffles split a job into stages. Inside a stage, cooks work alone ("narrow" steps). Between stages, they swap ingredients across the kitchen ("wide" steps). Fewer, smaller shuffles usually means a faster job.

    Inside the data centerROW 14 · RACK B

    When you start a cluster, Azure switches on real virtual machines in a data center near you. Your driver and each executor run on servers in racks, joined by high-speed network cables.

    🏢
    Where it lives
    In a compute hall of your Azure region. The driver and executors are separate VMs, often on different physical servers, sometimes in different racks.
    🖥️
    The machines
    For heavy joins, a memory-optimised VM such as Standard_E8ds_v5: 8 vCPUs, 64 GiB of RAM and a local SSD, a slice of a dual-socket rack server with many cores, lots of memory and fast network cards.
    🔀
    The physical journey
    Your notebook → the control plane → the driver VM → tasks to executor VMs. Each executor pulls its partitions from ADLS Gen2 storage servers, across the network, into memory. During a shuffle, data rushes between servers through the top-of-rack switches and up to the spine switches when executors sit in different racks.
    ⚡
    Power & heat
    A busy rack of general-purpose servers often draws somewhere between 5 and 20 kW, as much as several homes, all turned into heat for the cooling system to remove.
    📏
    Scale
    A standard rack has 42 slots (rack units). A typical 1U or 2U server takes one or two, so one rack can hold a few dozen servers, each hosting several VMs.
    🛠️
    When something breaks
    If a server dies mid-job, its executor is lost. The driver notices, and Spark reruns just the lost tasks on the remaining executors; Databricks requests a replacement VM. Your job finishes a little later instead of failing.
    👂
    Feel it

    Spark's results don't go through the driver unless you ask. .write lets every executor save its own slice straight to storage in parallel. .collect() pulls everything into the driver's memory, the classic way to crash a driver with a big table.

    Chapter 6 recap

    • The driver plans; executors do the work, one task per partition per core.
    • Transformations are lazy; actions make Spark run the whole optimised plan.
    • Shuffles move data across the network between stages, and they are the expensive part.
    • More executors help only up to the number of partitions, and shuffles grow with cluster size.
