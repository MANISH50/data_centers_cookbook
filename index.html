    The cloud has an address

    What a data center is, and why the internet can't live without them.

    Photo: Tony Webster from Minneapolis, Minnesota, United States · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

    You pay ₹20 for a cup of tea with UPI. Before the shopkeeper's speaker says "payment received", two banks have checked, agreed and saved your payment. Where did all that happen?

    Not in your phone. Not in the shop. It happened inside a few big buildings full of computers, maybe hundreds of kilometres away. Those buildings are data centers.

    The big idea

    A factory for information

    A is a building made to hold thousands of . A server is like a very powerful computer that never sleeps.

    Like a factory, it runs all day and night. But instead of making cars or biscuits, it does three jobs with data:

    🗄️

    Store

    Keep your photos, chats and bank records safe.

    🔀

    Move

    Send data to the right person, fast.

    ⚙️

    Compute

    Do the thinking: search, maths, AI answers.

    Why not just use your phone?

    Your phone is small, runs on a battery and has room for a few thousand photos. YouTube receives far more new video than that every single minute. No phone could hold it, let alone send it to millions of people at once.

    So we share. Millions of people use the same giant, always-on computers, and our phones just show the results. That shared pool of computers is what people call .

    The cloud isn't in the sky. It's in a warehouse with very good air conditioning.

    The rack

    A bookshelf for computers

    Servers don't sit on desks. They slide into a : a steel cabinet about 2 metres tall and 60 cm wide. Servers stack inside it like books on a shelf.

    Height is measured in . One U is 4.45 cm, and a standard rack has 42 of them. A slim server takes 1U; a big AI server might take 8U or more. In the photo, you can see the unit numbers 33–36 printed on the rack's edge.

    Storage

    Where your photos actually live

    Data centers keep data on different kinds of storage. Each one is a trade-off between speed and cost, just like at home.

    🧠

    Your desk. Super fast, small, and wiped when power goes off. Holds what is being worked on right now.

    ⚡

    A filing cabinet that opens instantly. Fast flash chips, no moving parts. Used for databases and busy apps.

    💿

    A big warehouse. Spinning disks, slower but much cheaper per gigabyte. Holds huge piles of photos and videos.

    📼

    Tape

    A deep basement archive. Yes, tape still exists! One LTO-10 cartridge holds up to 40 TB and can sit safely for decades.

    Never just one copy

    Drives fail. Every day, somewhere in a large data center, a drive dies. So your data is always stored more than once: on several drives (called ), on several servers, and often in another building too.

    When a drive breaks, a technician swaps it and the copies quietly rebuild. You never notice.

    Networking

    The roads between the computers

    Every server connects to a , usually at the top of its rack. Think of the switch as a small post office for that rack.

    Inside the building, data travels as over copper cables for short hops and cables (glass threads that carry light) for longer ones.

    Spine and leaf: no server is far away

    Big data centers use a design. Each rack's "leaf" switch connects to every "spine" switch. So any server can reach any other in just a few hops, and if one spine fails, the others carry on.

    Hover or tap a leaf switch to see its paths to every spine.

    Most traffic inside a big data center never leaves the building. Servers talk to each other far more than they talk to you. Cisco once estimated that about 70% of data center traffic stays inside. Engineers call this .

    Real life

    When a Databricks job reads a huge sales table, the data isn't on the computer doing the maths. It sits in in the same cloud region, and flows across this network to the workers. See it happen in Chapter 10.

    Chapter 2 recap

    • Most of a data center's floor space supports the computers: power, cooling, network and security.
    • Servers sit in 42U racks, stacked like books.
    • Data lives on RAM, SSDs, hard disks and tape, always with extra copies.
    • Switches and fibre connect everything; spine-leaf keeps every server a few hops away.

    Chapter 3: Meet the servers →

    03

    Computers that never sleep

    Rack, blade, tower, database, storage, AI, edge: meet every kind of server, then look inside.

    Photo: Victor Grigas · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    A server is like a very powerful computer that never sleeps. But "server" is like the word "vehicle": scooters, buses and trucks are all vehicles, built for very different jobs.

    A server is any computer whose job is to serve: it waits for requests ("show me this video", "save this payment") and answers them. Servers are described in two ways: by their shape (how they're built) and by their job (what they do).

    Part 1 · Shapes

    Three ways to build a server

    Rack server

    A flat "pizza box" that slides into a rack. 1U or 2U thick. The most common server in the world.

    Blade server

    Thin "blades" slot into a shared chassis, like books between bookends. They share power, fans and network, so you fit more in less space.

    Tower server

    Looks like a big desktop PC that stands on the floor. Perfect for a small shop or office with no server room.

    Part 2 · Try it

    Rack server

    The all-rounder. Flat, stackable, and easy to swap out.

    Built for
    Almost anything: websites, apps, small databases, virtual machines.
    Where
    Every kind of data center, from a company server room to a hyperscale hall.
    Key parts
    1–2 CPUs, 64 GB–2 TB of RAM, a few SSDs, 2 power supplies, a row of fans.
    Typical power

    Cooling & water

    Everyday example
    The server that sends you your bank's login page.

    Blade server

    Many thin computers sharing one box.

    Built for
    Packing lots of computing into a small space, with less cabling.
    Where
    Banks, telecom companies and large company data centers.
    Key parts
    Each blade: CPUs, RAM, maybe one or two drives. The chassis: shared power supplies, fans and network switches.
    Typical power
    A full chassis of 8–16 blades can draw several kilowatts.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Air-cooled through shared fans. Packing more computing into less space concentrates the heat, so the building's cooling (and any water it uses) works harder per rack.
    Everyday example
    A mobile operator's billing system, crunching millions of call records.

    Tower server

    A server that looks like a PC, for places without racks.

    Built for
    Small offices, shops, clinics and schools.
    Where
    Under a desk or in a cupboard, not usually in big data centers.
    Key parts
    1 CPU, modest RAM, a few hard disks, often just one power supply.
    Typical power
    About 150–500 W.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Cooled by room air or an office AC. Almost no water on site.
    Everyday example
    The machine running a pharmacy's stock and billing software.

    Web & application servers

    The front counter and the kitchen of every app.

    Built for
    A web server receives your request and sends back pages and pictures. An application server runs the business logic: "is this seat free?", "apply the coupon".
    Where
    Hundreds or thousands of identical rack servers behind a .
    Key parts
    Strong CPUs and plenty of RAM. Little storage: they keep data in databases.
    Typical power
    About 300–600 W each.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Air-cooled. Its water footprint comes from the building's cooling system and from the power station that makes its electricity.
    Everyday example
    When you open a shopping app, a web server sends the page and an app server works out your cart total.

    Database server

    The memory of the business. Never forgets, never mixes up.

    Built for
    Storing records and answering questions like "what is Priya's balance?" thousands of times a second, without mistakes.
    Where
    Banks, shops, hospitals, airlines: anywhere records matter. Usually in pairs, with a live copy ready to take over.
    Key parts
    Lots of RAM (often 1 TB or more), very fast SSDs, strong CPUs.
    Typical power
    About 600–1,500 W.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Air-cooled. Busy around the clock, so it keeps the cooling system (and its water use) busy too.
    Everyday example
    Your UPI payment ends with a database writing "₹20 moved from A to B".

    Storage server

    A server that is mostly drives.

    Built for
    Holding huge amounts of files: photos, videos, backups, data lakes.
    Where
    Cloud storage (like Google Drive or Amazon S3), video platforms, backup systems.
    Key parts
    Dozens of hard disks or SSDs (some boxes hold 60 or more), modest CPUs, fast network cards.
    Typical power
    About 500–1,500 W, mostly from the spinning disks.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Air-cooled. Disks need steady, moderate temperatures; their heat ends up in the building's cooling system.
    Everyday example
    Where your phone's photo backup actually ends up.

    AI server (GPU server)

    A heavy-duty machine built around 8 powerful GPUs.

    Built for
    Training AI models and answering AI questions ().
    Where
    AI clusters at cloud providers and AI companies, often thousands of these linked together.
    Key parts
    8 , each with its own super-fast memory; 2 CPUs; terabytes of RAM; one very fast network link per GPU; many large power supplies.
    Typical power
    About 10–15 kW. NVIDIA's DGX B300 is rated at about 14.5 kW, as much as 20 to 30 normal servers.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Newer AI servers are often liquid-cooled with a closed loop: the same coolant goes round and round. Water is only used up if the building gets rid of the heat with evaporative cooling towers.
    Everyday example
    The machine that writes a chatbot's reply to you, word by word.

    Edge server

    A small server placed close to people, not in a far-away building.

    Built for
    Jobs that need very fast answers or save long trips: video caches, factory robots, shop cameras, 5G networks.
    Where
    At phone towers, inside internet providers' buildings, in factories and shops. Many are rugged, short and quiet.
    Key parts
    Compact CPU (sometimes a small GPU), SSDs, network cards; built to handle dust and heat.
    Typical power
    About 100–500 W.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Usually cooled by fans and outside air in a sealed box. Uses almost no water.
    Everyday example
    The cache near you that starts your YouTube video in a blink.

    Mainframe

    The armoured truck of computing: huge, ultra-reliable, very secure.

    Built for
    Massive numbers of small, critical transactions, with almost zero downtime.
    Where
    Big banks, insurers, airlines and governments.
    Key parts
    Many special processors, enormous memory, spare parts built in for every component, so faulty parts can be replaced while it runs.
    Typical power
    Several kW to tens of kW, depending on size: a fridge-sized cabinet doing the work of many servers.
    Cooling & water
    💧 Air-cooled, or cooled with a closed water loop connected to the building's chilled water.
    Everyday example
    Card payments and airline bookings often end up on a mainframe.
    Part 3 · Look inside

    Open the lid

    Take the cover off a server and you'll find the same family of parts as in a laptop, just bigger, doubled for safety and built to run for years. Tap the numbers.

    Rack server: what does each part do?

    1. CPU
    2. Memory (RAM)
    3. Storage drive
    4. Fans & heatsinks
    5. Power supply
    6. Network card

    1 · CPU (under the black air duct)

    The brain. It follows the program's instructions, billions per second. Servers have one or two, hidden under metal heatsinks; the duct steers the fans' air across them.

    2 · Memory (RAM)

    The desk. Holds everything the CPU is working on right now, so it doesn't have to fetch it from slow drives.

    3 · Storage drive

    The filing cabinet. Keeps data even when power is off. This older server has a spinning hard disk (you can see its green circuit board); modern servers mostly use SSDs.

    4 · Fans & heatsinks

    The cooling. Metal fins soak up heat from the chips; a row of fans blows cold air from front to back.

    5 · Power supply (PSU)

    Turns building power into the low, steady voltage chips need. Most servers have two, so one can fail safely.

    6 · Network card (NIC)

    The front door for data. Network ports sit at the back of the server (here, on the left), with extra cards in the expansion slots. Cables connect it to the rack switch.

    AI server: what's different?

    1. 8 GPUs
    2. Giant heatsinks
    3. GPU-to-GPU links
    4. The baseboard

    1 · Eight GPUs, side by side

    Each tall block hides one GPU with its own ultra-fast memory stacked right next to it. Eight is the standard number in one AI server.

    2 · Giant heatsinks

    Each GPU can use about 1,000 W or more, so it needs huge metal fins (or liquid-cooled cold plates) to stay cool.

    3 · GPU-to-GPU links

    Between the two rows of GPUs sit switch chips that connect all 8 GPUs with , so they can share work as if they were one giant chip. The sign says "NVL8": 8 GPUs linked.

    4 · The baseboard

    All 8 GPUs sit on one big board with heavy power connectors along the edge. This tray slides into the server; the rest of the box holds 2 CPUs, RAM, network cards and a wall of power supplies.

    Blade chassis: sharing is the trick

    1. Blades
    2. Shared fans
    3. Shared switches
    4. Shared power

    1 · The blades

    Each blade is a full computer (CPUs, RAM, maybe a drive) without its own fans or power supply. Pull one out while the others keep running.

    2 · Shared fans

    A few big fans at the back cool every blade, more efficiently than many tiny fans.

    3 · Shared network switches

    Blades connect to switches built into the chassis, so you need far fewer cables.

    4 · Shared power supplies

    Several large power supplies feed all the blades, with spares in case one fails.

    Part 4 · AI servers

    Why does AI need GPUs?

    A is like a few brilliant chefs: each can cook any dish, but they work one dish at a time. A is like a stadium crowd holding coloured cards: each person does one tiny, simple job, but thousands do it at the same moment.

    AI models are mostly one simple thing: multiplying huge grids of numbers, billions of times. Each multiplication doesn't depend on the others. That's a perfect job for a crowd.

    What changes for AI?

    A website looks things up. A chatbot works things out.

    A normal page is mostly fetched: the answer already exists in a database. An AI answer doesn't exist until a model writes it, one at a time, on servers.

    That's why chatbot answers appear word by word. The words are being made while you watch, and streamed to you as they come.

    0 The energy Google measured for a typical (median) Gemini text question: about the same as watching TV for under 9 seconds. It also used about 0.26 ml of water, roughly five drops, for cooling.     Light in fibre travels at about 200,000 km per second. A request from Mumbai to a data center in Singapore and back (about 8,000 km of cable) needs at least 40 milliseconds just for the light to make the trip. That's why companies build data centers close to their users.

    Chapter 4 recap

    • DNS finds the address; routers carry packets across the internet.
    • Inside: firewall → load balancer → web server → app server → database, then back.
    • A normal page takes about a tenth of a second or two.
    • AI answers are generated token by token on GPUs and streamed back as they're made. Each one costs a little energy and a few drops of cooling water.

    All of this needs one thing, every millisecond, without a single gap: electricity.
    Chapter 5: Power →

    05

    Never, ever switch off

    Grid, batteries, generators: the relay race that keeps every server running through a blackout.

    Photo: Robert.Harker · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    The lights in your home flicker during a storm. Your laptop doesn't care: it has a battery. Now imagine 50,000 servers, a bank's payments and a hospital's records, with no room for even one flicker.

    A data center's power system has one rule: the servers must never notice when anything goes wrong. To pull that off, power arrives by more than one road, and there is always a backup for the backup.

    The power chain

    From the power station to the chip

    Electricity changes shape several times on its way in. Scroll sideways to follow it.

    🏭GridPower lines carry very high voltage from power stations.
    🔌Steps the voltage down. Big sites often have their own.
    🔄Lowers it again, to the 400-ish volts the building uses.
    🎛️ + Giant switches and breakers. The ATS can swap the grid for generators.
    🔋Cleans the power and holds batteries ready for instant backup.
    📶Splits power into many circuits, one for each rack.
    🔌Rack PDUA heavy-duty power strip inside each rack.
    🖥️Server Turns it into the low voltage (around 12 volts) chips need.
    Two roads in

    The A side and the B side

    Remember the two power supplies inside every server? One plugs into the A power path, the other into the B path. Each path has its own switchgear, UPS and PDUs.

    If anything on path A breaks, or needs repairs, path B carries the full load. This idea is called , and we'll see more of it in Chapter 8.

    Backup #1

    The UPS: zero-second backup

    In most data centers, the servers aren't fed straight from the grid. Power goes through the , which turns it into battery-style (DC) power and back again. So if the grid dies, the batteries are already connected. There is no gap at all.

    The batteries are only a bridge. They usually last 5 to 10 minutes at full load. Older sites use lead-acid batteries, like a car's; newer ones often use lithium-ion, like a phone's.

    Backup #2

    Generators: the marathon runners

    For longer cuts, big diesel take over. Each can be the size of a shipping container. They need about 10 seconds to start and settle at the right speed. Then an moves the load onto them.

    The Uptime Institute's design standard asks for at least 12 hours of fuel on site, and many sites store more. Generators are test-run regularly, because a backup you never test is just a hope.

    Chapter 5 recap

    • Power steps down from the grid through substation, transformer, switchgear, UPS and PDUs to each server.
    • Two independent paths, A and B, feed every server.
    • The UPS bridges the gap instantly; batteries last minutes.
    • Generators start in about 10 seconds and can run for many hours.

    All that electricity ends up somewhere: as heat. Lots and lots of heat.
    Chapter 6: Cooling →

    06

    A room full of hair-dryers

    Hot aisles, cold aisles and liquid: how data centers stop their computers from cooking.

    Photo: Robert.Harker · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    Almost every watt a server uses turns into heat. One modern AI rack gives off as much heat as about 90 room heaters, all switched on, all day, all year.

    Chips that get too hot slow themselves down, and if they stay hot, they fail. So keeping the air at the front of the servers cool is one of a data center's most important jobs. The ASHRAE guidelines the industry follows recommend air of about 18–27 °C going into servers: chilly for a person, comfy for a computer.

    The basic trick

    Hot aisle, cold aisle

    Servers suck in air at the front and blow hot air out the back. So racks are lined up front to front and back to back. The fronts share a ; the backs share a hot aisle.

    Many sites go further and put doors and a roof on one of the aisles (called containment), so hot and cold air can never mix. It's like keeping the fridge door closed.

    The floor that breathes

    In many older halls, the floor is raised about half a metre. Cold air from big air conditioners () is pushed into the space underneath. Tiles full of holes in the cold aisle let it rise right in front of the servers.

    Newer halls often skip the raised floor and drop cold air from above or blow it in from the side, but the idea is the same.

    Simplified physics: air warming by 12 °C, coolant by 10 °C, a room heater = 1.5 kW, and about 1.6 litres of water evaporated to carry away 1 kWh of heat. Limits are typical, not exact.

    Where does the heat go?

    Passing the heat outside

    1. Servers warm the air passing through them.
    2. CRAH units in the hall pull in the hot air and pass it over coils full of cold water.
    3. The now-warm water goes to a , a giant fridge that takes the heat out.
    4. Finally, or dry coolers on the roof release the heat into the outside air.

    In cool places, data centers use cold outside air or water directly for much of the year and barely run the chillers. That's called free cooling.

    💧 The water question

    Why computers drink water

    Servers don't use water directly. But many data centers get rid of their heat by evaporating it, the same way sweat cools your skin. In a , warm water trickles down while air blows through it. A little evaporates and carries a lot of heat away.

    Evaporating just 1 litre of water carries off about 0.63 kWh of heat. That's very efficient, which is why it's popular. But the evaporated water is gone into the sky, and it's usually clean drinking-quality water.

    🌫️

    Direct (on-site) water

    Water evaporated by cooling towers or sprayed to cool incoming air. Measured as : litres per kWh of computing.

    🏭

    Indirect water

    Coal, gas and nuclear power stations also evaporate water to cool themselves, often 1–3 litres per kWh. Wind and solar need almost none.

    🔁

    Closed loops

    Liquid-cooled racks pump the same coolant round and round. If the heat then leaves through dry coolers, almost no water is used up.

    ⚖️

    The trade-off

    Skipping evaporation saves water but often costs more electricity, especially in hot weather. Engineers balance the two based on the local climate and water supply.

    The AI era

    When air isn't enough: liquid

    Water can carry about 3,500 times more heat than the same amount of air. That's why a cold bath cools you much faster than a cold room.

    (direct-to-chip): a metal block with coolant flowing through it sits on top of each hot chip. This is how racks like NVIDIA's GB300 NVL72 are cooled.

    Google's data center in Hamina, Finland, is cooled with seawater from the Gulf of Finland, pumped through an old paper mill's tunnels.

    Real life

    Every chatbot answer you read ends up as a little warmth in a coolant loop. The energy used to "think" leaves the building as heat, carried away by air, water or both.

    Chapter 6 recap

    • Nearly all the power a server uses becomes heat.
    • Hot and cold aisles keep hot and cold air apart; containment seals them.
    • Heat moves from servers → CRAH → chilled water → chiller → cooling tower.
    • AI racks (100+ kW) need liquid: cold plates or immersion.
    • Cooling towers evaporate water to dump heat; closed loops and dry coolers save water but can cost energy.

    Harder to enter than an airport

    Getting into some data centers takes longer than boarding an international flight. Even the people who work there need a badge, a fingerprint and permission for each room.

    That's because the most valuable thing in the building isn't the hardware. It's the data: bank balances, health records, private messages. So security works like an onion, layer after layer. Walk through them.

    Try it

    Get to the server

    Even inside

    Locked cages and destroyed drives

    In shared () buildings, each customer's racks sit inside their own locked cage. Cameras watch every aisle, and every door opening is logged.

    When a drive gets old, it isn't thrown away. Big operators wipe it and then physically crush or shred it on site, so no data can ever leave the building on a forgotten disk.

    Fire

    Fighting fire without water

    Water and electronics don't mix. So many server rooms put fires out with gas. Special "clean agents" either soak up the heat or lower the oxygen just enough to stop the flames, and leave no mess behind.

    It all starts with smell. pipes sip air from the room nonstop and can catch smoke from an overheating cable long before you could see it.

    Try it

    How a fire is stopped

    1. Sniff. VESDA notices a tiny amount of smoke in the air.
    2. Confirm. A second detector must agree, so one false alarm can't trigger anything.
    3. Warn. Alarms and lights tell people to leave; doors close to keep the gas in.
    4. Suppress. Gas floods the room in about 10 seconds. The fire goes out.
    5. Backup. If it still burns, a "pre-action" sprinkler releases water only above the fire.
    Monitoring

    The room that watches everything

    Thousands of sensors report every few seconds: air temperature at each rack, humidity, power on every circuit, water leaks under the floor, every door opening.

    It all flows into software called and onto the big screens of the , staffed 24 hours a day. Most problems are fixed before any customer notices.

    Chapter 8: Redundancy and tiers →

    08

    A spare for everything

    N+1, 2N, "five nines" and the Tier I–IV ratings: how data centers plan for things to break.

    Photo: Victor Grigas · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    99.9% sounds perfect, doesn't it? In a year, it still allows almost 9 hours of downtime. For a payment network, that's millions of failed payments.

    Everything breaks eventually: fans, drives, power supplies, whole cooling units. A data center can't stop that. Instead, it makes sure that when one thing fails, something else is already there. That's called .

    The enemy has a name: the single point of failure. That's any one part whose failure takes everything down. Designers hunt them down, one by one.

    N

    Exactly what you need. If the building needs 4 cooling units, it has 4. One fails, and you're in trouble.

    One spare. 5 units for a job that needs 4. Any one can fail, or be serviced, and you're fine.

    A full second copy. Two complete, separate systems (A and B), each able to carry everything.

    2N+1

    Two full copies plus a spare. For places where failure is simply not an option.

    Uptime: counting the nines

    The rating system

    Tier I to Tier IV

    The Uptime Institute rates data center designs in four . Higher tiers survive more problems, and cost much more to build.

    Tier I

    Basic

    One path for power and cooling, no spares. Any repair means a shutdown.

    99.671%≈ 28.8 h down / year
    Tier II

    Spare parts

    Still one path, but with spare components (like N+1 generators and UPS modules).

    99.741%≈ 22 h down / year
    Tier III

    Fix it while running

    "Concurrently maintainable": every part can be taken out for repair without shutting down, thanks to a second path.

    99.982%≈ 1.6 h down / year
    Tier IV

    Fault tolerant

    Survives any single failure, even a fire in one room, automatically. Fully duplicated (2N) systems.

    99.995%≈ 26 min down / year

    The percentages are widely used rules of thumb linked to each tier. The official standard judges the design itself, not a number.

    0 The yearly downtime commonly linked to a Tier IV design: less than one episode of a TV show, in a whole year.

    The biggest single point of failure is often the whole building. That's why banks, payment networks and cloud companies run a second data center far away (a disaster recovery site, or another ) that can take over if the first is hit by a flood, a fire or a power failure across the city.

    Real life

    Your UPI payment touches several systems, each with spares: two power paths, spare cooling, mirrored databases and backup sites. That's how a network handling over 20 billion payments a month stays up.

    • Redundancy means a spare is ready before anything fails.
    • N = no spare, N+1 = one spare, 2N = a full second copy.
    • Each extra "nine" of uptime cuts the yearly downtime by ten.
    • Tier I–IV rate designs, from basic to fully fault-tolerant.

    Chapter 11: Energy, water and the planet →

    11

    Every watt, every drop

    PUE, WUE, clean power and waste heat: how efficient data centers are, and how they're getting greener.

    Photo: Diliff · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

    For every ₹100 of electricity a data center buys, how much actually reaches the computers? In an average data center, about ₹65. In the best ones, over ₹90.

    The rest goes to cooling, to power losses in UPS systems and transformers, and to lights. The industry measures this with one simple number: .

    PUE = total power the building usespower used by the IT equipment

    A PUE of 1.0 would be perfect: every watt goes to computing. The world average has been stuck around 1.54 for years (Uptime Institute, 2025). Google reports 1.09 across its fleet for 2024.

    Rough teaching model. Assumes: evaporative WUE ≈ 1.0 L/kWh, dry ≈ 0.05, closed-loop liquid ≈ 0.02; power stations ≈ 2 L of water per kWh from non-wind/solar sources; an Indian household ≈ 1,200 kWh a year. Real sites vary a lot with climate.

    Clean power

    Buying the wind and the sun

    Big operators sign long contracts to buy power from new wind and solar farms, often in the same region as their data centers. Google's campus in Oklahoma (pictured), for example, buys power from wind farms in the same state. Some aim higher: Google's goal is to run on carbon-free energy every hour of every day by 2030, not just on average over a year.

    That's hard, because the wind doesn't always blow when servers are busy. So the next steps are batteries, smart scheduling (running flexible jobs when clean power is plentiful) and new sources like geothermal and nuclear.

    Waste heat

    Heating homes with servers

    All that heat doesn't have to be thrown away. In Nordic cities, from data centers is pumped into district heating: networks of hot-water pipes that warm whole neighbourhoods.

    Stockholm has a city programme for it, and in the Helsinki area, heat from new Microsoft data centers is planned to warm homes through the local energy company's network.

    💧 Water, from the top

    The full water footprint of computing

    1. On site: cooling towers and evaporative coolers (see Chapter 6). Measured by WUE: big cloud companies report about 0.2–0.3 litres per kWh; hot, dry sites using evaporation can need several.
    2. At the power station: coal, gas and nuclear plants evaporate water for their own cooling. Wind and solar power cut this to almost nothing.
    3. In the factory: making chips needs huge amounts of ultra-pure water. It's part of every server's footprint before it is even switched on.

    That's why where a data center is built matters. The same server can have a tiny water footprint in a cool, rainy place with clean power, and a big one in a hot, dry place running on coal.

    0 terawatt-hours: the electricity data centers may use in 2030, more than double 2024. Efficiency isn't optional anymore. Source: IEA, Energy and AI (2025), base case

    The most efficient watt is the one you never use. Shutting down idle servers, running fewer, busier machines (virtualization!), and newer chips that do more work per watt often save more than any fancy cooling trick.

    Chapter 11 recap

    • PUE = total power ÷ IT power. Average ≈ 1.54; the best ≈ 1.1.
    • WUE measures on-site water per kWh; power stations and chip factories add more.
    • Saving water and saving energy can pull in opposite directions; climate decides the balance.
    • Clean power contracts, heat reuse and busier servers make data centers greener.

    Years before the first server

    Choosing the land, building the shell, testing everything, then running it 24 hours a day.

    Photo: Lapalmauz · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

    Before the first server arrives, someone has spent years choosing a patch of land, and fighting for enough electricity to power a small city.

    Where a data center goes decides almost everything else: how much it costs, how fast it is, how much water and carbon it uses, and how safe it is. Planners juggle a long checklist.

    ⚡

    Power

    Can the grid supply 50, 100 or 500 MW? Today this is often the hardest question, with waits of several years for a big connection.

    🧵

    Fibre

    Several separate fibre routes in, so one cut cable can't cut the site off.

    💧

    Water

    Is there water to spare for cooling, or is the area already short of it? If so, the site must use dry or closed-loop cooling.

    🌊

    Risks

    Floods, earthquakes, cyclones, wildfires, even flight paths. Planners check maps going back a century.

    🌡️

    Climate

    Cool air means more free cooling and lower PUE. Hot, humid places need more cooling energy (and often water).

    📍

    Distance to users

    Closer means faster. Light in fibre needs about 1 ms for every 100 km, each way.

    From empty field to live

    How a data center gets built

    1. Land and power (1–3+ years). Buy or lease the land, and request a grid connection. The power queue is often the slowest step of all.
    2. Design and permits. Engineers design the power, cooling, security and fire systems; councils check noise, water, traffic and the environment.
    3. Build the shell (about 1–2 years). Foundations, steel, walls, roof. Many builders now use factory-made modules (power rooms, cooling skids) to save time.
    4. Fit out. Transformers, switchgear, UPS, batteries, generators, chillers and miles of cable go in. Big transformers and generators can take a year or more just to be delivered.
    5. Commissioning: test everything. Testing happens level by level, from single machines up to the whole site. Fake "servers" called load banks make heat and draw power, and engineers pull the plug on the grid to prove the backups work.
    6. Go live. Servers arrive at the loading dock, are unboxed, racked, cabled, tested and switched on, one hall at a time.
    Commissioning

    Testing with fake servers

    A load bank is a big box of heaters and fans that pretends to be thousands of servers. It lets engineers run generators, UPS and cooling at full power, for hours, before a single real customer moves in.

    The final exam is the integrated systems test: everything runs together while engineers cut the grid, fail a cooling unit, or switch off one side of power, and check that nothing hiccups.

    Running it

    A day in the life

    A big data center runs every minute of every year with a surprisingly small team: facility engineers (electrical and mechanical), security officers, technicians, and control-room operators. Here's a typical day.

    1. 07:00Shift handover. The night team briefs the day team: any alarms, open tickets, planned work.
    2. 08:00Walk-around. Engineers walk every hall and plant room: checking temperatures and gauges, listening for odd noises, looking for leaks.
    3. 10:00"Remote hands" ticket. A customer's drive has failed. A technician finds the exact rack, swaps the hot-swap drive in two minutes, and the data rebuilds automatically.
    4. 13:00Generator test. One generator is started and run under load, as scheduled. Fuel, oil and batteries are checked.
    5. 15:00Deliveries. Pallets of new servers arrive. They are checked, unpacked away from the halls (no cardboard dust!), and moved in for racking.
    6. 18:00Planned maintenance. A UPS module is serviced, following a step-by-step written procedure, while the other side carries the load.
    7. 02:00Night alarm. A cooling unit reports a fault. The spare has already taken over; the engineer confirms, logs it and books a repair. Customers notice nothing.

    Most outages aren't caused by storms or fires. Surveys by the Uptime Institute keep finding that human error plays a part in a large share of serious incidents, usually because a procedure wasn't followed or wasn't good enough. That's why data center work is run on checklists, like flying a plane.

    Chapter 12 recap

    • Location is chosen for power, fibre, water, safety, climate and closeness to users.
    • Getting a big grid connection is often the slowest part.
    • Everything is tested at full load, with fake servers, before going live.
    • A small team runs it 24/7 using walk-arounds, procedures and the control room.
